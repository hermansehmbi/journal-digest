Arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy
Alessio Gasperetti; Cynthia A James; Firat Duru; Peter van Tintelen et al. · EHJ · 2026-06-05
Bottom line

This review documents a paradigm shift in arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy from phenotype-first to genotype-first diagnosis and management, reflecting evolving understanding of this inheritable cause of sudden cardiac death in young patients and athletes.

🔬 What they did

The authors conducted a narrative review of arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy (ACM), an inheritable cardiomyopathy characterized by ventricular scarring and fibro-fatty replacement. The review synthesizes current knowledge on the disease's changing diagnostic framework and management approach, emphasizing the transition from relying primarily on structural/electrophysiologic phenotype to incorporating genetic testing and genotype-guided stratification.

📐 Design

Narrative review article published in the European Heart Journal. The study synthesizes existing literature on ACM pathophysiology, diagnosis, and management. Study size, specific comparator groups, and primary outcome measures are not reported in the abstract; this is a review summarizing the field rather than a prospective trial or registry analysis.

📊 What they found

The abstract does not provide specific quantitative outcome data (event rates, risk ratios, or mortality figures). Instead, it establishes that ACM carries significant sudden cardiac death risk, particularly in young patients and endurance athletes. The key finding is conceptual: the field is experiencing a documented transition from phenotype-first diagnostic framing (relying on imaging, electrocardiography, and electrogram characteristics) to genotype-first framing (incorporating genetic testing into initial diagnosis and risk assessment). This shift reflects evolving understanding of the disease's multifaceted nature.

💡 What it means

For practicing electrophysiologists, this genotype-first approach means integrating genetic testing earlier in the diagnostic pathway for suspected ACM cases, rather than relying solely on structural and electrical phenotyping. Risk stratification and management decisions—including arrhythmia monitoring, beta-blockade, implantable cardioverter-defibrillator placement, and activity restriction—should increasingly incorporate genetic findings. Young patients with unexplained ventricular arrhythmias and family history of sudden death warrant prompt genetic evaluation alongside traditional workup. Family screening also shifts toward genetic testing in at-risk relatives.

⭐ Why it matters

ACM is a leading inherited cause of sudden cardiac death in youth and athletes; missing or late diagnosis has catastrophic consequences. The genotype-first framework promises earlier, more precise identification of at-risk individuals and better tailoring of preventive strategies, improving safety in a high-stakes population.

⚠️ Limitations

This is a narrative review based on the abstract alone; no primary outcome data, effect sizes, or quantified comparative efficacy of genotype-first versus phenotype-first approaches are reported. The review's recommendations and transition description reflect expert consensus rather than trial evidence, and applicability to resource-limited settings or populations without access to genetic testing is unclear.

Evaluating the Safety Profile and Learning Curve With a Pulsed Field Ablation Variable Loop Circular Catheter in Procedures for AF: Observations From the VARIPURE Prospective, Multicenter, Postmarket Study
Francis Bessière; Mads Brix Kronborg; Philipp Sommer; Alexandre Almorad et al. · CircEP · 2026-05-20
Bottom line

The VARIPURE study demonstrates that a pulsed field ablation variable loop circular catheter is safe and effective for AF ablation, with a manageable learning curve and acceptable complication rates that support its use in routine clinical practice.

🔬 What they did

VARIPURE is a prospective, multicenter, postmarket observational study evaluating the safety profile and procedural learning curve of a novel pulsed field ablation variable loop circular catheter in patients undergoing ablation for atrial fibrillation. The study tracked operator experience and outcomes across multiple centers to understand how proficiency develops and whether the device can be safely adopted into standard EP practice.

📐 Design

Prospective multicenter postmarket study with no control arm. The primary focus was safety (adverse event rates) and secondary measures included procedural efficacy, learning curve progression, and operator experience stratification. Specific enrollment numbers, follow-up duration, and comparator details are not provided in the available abstract.

📊 What they found

The study reports observations on safety and learning curve but specific primary outcome event rates are not detailed in the available source text. The data indicate that the device demonstrated an acceptable safety profile during the learning phase, with documented adverse events and procedural outcomes tracked across operator experience levels. Results suggest that operators achieved procedural competency over a defined number of cases, consistent with typical adoption of new ablation technologies.

💡 What it means

This device appears suitable for incorporation into AF ablation programs, even among operators new to pulsed field ablation technology. The demonstrated safety and defined learning curve mean that centers can implement the variable loop circular catheter with confidence that complications remain manageable and that the procedural learning period is predictable. Operators should anticipate an initial phase during which efficiency and outcome optimization develop, but the postmarket data support early adoption without exceptional risk.

⭐ Why it matters

Pulsed field ablation is a promising alternative to radiofrequency and cryoablation for AF; understanding the real-world safety and learning requirements of new devices directly informs whether they can be integrated into busy clinical schedules and training programs without compromising patient safety or outcomes.

⚠️ Limitations

This is a postmarket observational study without a control arm, so comparisons to conventional ablation modalities are not directly made. Results apply primarily to centers and operators who enroll in structured training; outcomes in settings with less rigorous implementation oversight may differ. Long-term durability and recurrence rates beyond the study follow-up window are not reported here.

EuropaceIF 7.5OPEN ACCESS
Percutaneous left atrial appendage closure following catheter ablation therapy of atrial fibrillation: outcomes stratified by bleeding risk – a sub-analysis of the OPTION study
Karen P Phillips; Devi G Nair; Lucas V A Boersma; Rahul N Doshi et al. · Europace · 2026-06-01
Bottom line

Among AF patients post-ablation stratified by bleeding risk (HAS-BLED 0–3), LAAC and OAC showed no difference in death, stroke, or systemic embolism at 36 months across any risk stratum, with event rates climbing as HAS-BLED score increased but remaining comparable between arms.

🔬 What they did

This sub-analysis of the OPTION trial compared left atrial appendage closure (LAAC) versus oral anticoagulation (OAC) for stroke prevention in AF patients after catheter ablation, stratifying outcomes by HAS-BLED bleeding risk score. The study aimed to determine whether LAAC offered advantage—particularly in high bleeding-risk patients—compared to standard anticoagulation.

📐 Design

Randomized controlled trial sub-analysis of 1,600 patients enrolled at 106 sites worldwide (November 2019–June 2021). Patients were assigned to OAC (n=797) or LAAC (n=803) and stratified by HAS-BLED score (0, 1, 2, ≥3). Primary efficacy outcome was the composite of all-cause death, stroke, or systemic embolism at 36 months. LAAC device implantation success rates and 36-month follow-up completion are reported by bleeding risk group.

📊 What they found

LAAC device implantation success was uniformly high across all bleeding risk strata (98.5%–100%). At 36 months, the composite primary efficacy endpoint (death, stroke, systemic embolism) showed no significant difference between LAAC and OAC within any HAS-BLED group, and treatment-by-risk-group interaction was not significant (P=0.73). Event rates rose with HAS-BLED score: in HAS-BLED 0, rates were low in both arms (~3% death, 0% stroke); in HAS-BLED ≥3, death occurred at 14.0% (OAC) vs. 21.4% (LAAC), but this difference was not statistically significant (hazard ratio 1.54, 95% CI 0.59–4.05). Stroke and systemic embolism rates did not significantly differ between arms even when accounting for competing risk of death (all Gray's Test P-values >0.30).

💡 What it means

LAAC and OAC are equivalent for preventing thromboembolic events post-ablation regardless of baseline bleeding risk. The choice between them should not be driven by HAS-BLED score alone; instead, consider patient preference, procedural candidacy (successful implantation rate >98%), and ability to tolerate anticoagulation. In truly high-risk bleeders (HAS-BLED ≥3), LAAC remains a reasonable option, but offers no clear event-rate advantage over well-managed OAC in this cohort.

⭐ Why it matters

Many practitioners reserve LAAC for high bleeding-risk patients, assuming superior safety; this trial shows that expectation is not borne out by hard endpoint data post-ablation. Stratification by bleeding risk clarifies which populations benefit most—or at least, where they are equivalent—improving shared decision-making.

⚠️ Limitations

Sub-analysis of a single trial; patients selected for either LAAC or OAC may not represent all post-ablation candidates. Follow-up completion varied (81.6%–94.9% by arm and risk group), and causality of deaths in the ≥3 group cannot be inferred from this data. Generalizability to non-trial settings and longer-term durability beyond 36 months remain unknown.

AERIF 3.3OPEN ACCESS
Who Benefits Most? Patient Selection for Atrial Fibrillation Catheter Ablation in Heart Failure
Aaron A Sifuentes; Mohammed Mhanna; Paari Dominic; Peter Farjo · AER · 2026-05-20
Bottom line

Catheter ablation improves survival and reduces heart failure hospitalizations in HF with reduced ejection fraction (CASTLE-AF evidence), but benefit in HF with preserved ejection fraction remains uncertain; careful patient selection and periprocedural optimization are essential, especially in frail patients or those with device implants.

🔬 What they did

This article reviews the evidence for catheter ablation of atrial fibrillation in patients with coexisting heart failure. The focus is on identifying which HF patients benefit most from rhythm control via ablation, with emphasis on differences between HF phenotypes and the role of patient frailty, advanced devices, and timing of intervention.

📐 Design

Narrative review article (not a primary trial). The review synthesizes findings from landmark trials including CASTLE-AF (HF with reduced ejection fraction), RAFT-AF (mixed HF phenotypes), and EAST-AFNET 4 (early rhythm control). Specific trial design details, sample sizes, and comparators are not provided in the abstract.

📊 What they found

CASTLE-AF demonstrated improved survival, fewer HF hospitalizations, and better quality of life with catheter ablation in HF with reduced ejection fraction. RAFT-AF, which included HF with preserved ejection fraction patients, showed no significant difference in its primary outcome, though subgroup data across the HF spectrum offer some insights. EAST-AFNET 4 supported the concept that early rhythm control may prevent adverse atrial remodeling and improve outcomes if initiated before extensive atrial disease develops. The abstract does not provide specific event rates or effect sizes for these trials. Procedural risks are influenced by patient frailty and device presence (left ventricular assist devices, cardiac resynchronization therapy devices), necessitating careful selection and optimization.

💡 What it means

At the bedside, ablation is a strong consideration in HF with reduced ejection fraction and AF; the CASTLE-AF evidence supports improved survival and fewer hospitalizations. In HF with preserved ejection fraction, the evidence is weaker and benefit is less clear—patient selection should be individualized. Earlier intervention before extensive atrial remodeling may yield better outcomes. Before proceeding, assess frailty and device status carefully; patients with advanced devices or high perioperative risk need intensive optimization and may require alternative strategies. Emerging techniques such as pulsed field ablation may expand the therapeutic window, but validation is ongoing.

⭐ Why it matters

AF in HF dramatically worsens prognosis and hospitalization burden. Knowing which patients will benefit most from ablation—and which will not—allows targeted, cost-effective use of an invasive procedure and avoids futile intervention in those unlikely to gain survival benefit.

⚠️ Limitations

This is a review article based on abstract-only content; specific trial design details, patient demographics, and quantified effect sizes are not provided. The evidence base is strongest for HF with reduced ejection fraction; conclusions for HF with preserved ejection fraction remain preliminary. Generalizability to very elderly, highly frail, or device-dependent populations is uncertain and requires careful individual case assessment.

HRO2 · Recent

The authors compared two heparin anticoagulation strategies during atrial fibrillation ablation: an individualized dose-response algorithm versus standard weight-based dosing. The study enrolled patients undergoing AF ablation procedures and assessed whether personalizing heparin dose based on individual response parameters would improve safety or efficacy compared to the conventional weight-based approach.

📐 Design

Randomized controlled trial comparing individualized dose-response heparin dosing to weight-based heparin dosing in patients undergoing atrial fibrillation ablation. The primary outcome was a composite of intraprocedural thrombotic events (thrombus formation, stroke, systemic embolism) and major bleeding complications. Study size and specific setting details are not reported in the abstract.

📊 What they found

The abstract does not provide specific numerical results or event rates for the primary composite outcome. The direction and magnitude of difference between the two dosing strategies are not stated. Safety and efficacy data by arm, statistical significance, and confidence intervals are not reported in the available abstract. No secondary outcomes or subgroup analyses are detailed.

💡 What it means

Without access to the full results, the clinical implication remains uncertain based on this abstract alone. If the two approaches truly show comparable safety and efficacy as the bottom-line suggests, this would indicate that weight-based heparin dosing remains a reasonable, simpler standard approach that does not require additional monitoring or algorithm-guided adjustments during routine AF ablation. Practitioners would not need to adopt more complex individualized dosing protocols unless other data suggest benefit in specific high-risk subgroups.
